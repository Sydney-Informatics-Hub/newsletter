The Apollo GPU Cluster is Officially Launching This Month  

Apollo, the University of Sydney’s new GPU platform for research, is officially launching this month to support AI-driven research, advanced modelling, simulation and large-scale data analysis.
To mark the launch, Sydney Informatics Hub is hosting two online information sessions led by Michael Lynch, about Apollo, for anyone interested in learning more about the University's new GPU capabilities, including researchers, professional staff, and students.
During the 30-minute session, you'll learn: 
  • What Apollo is and the types of research it can support 
  • How researchers are using GPU technology in practice 
  • How to get started and where to find support 
Whether you are curious about AI, conducting data-intensive research, or exploring more powerful computing options, this session will help you understand what is possible and where to get support.
Session dates:
  • 17 September, 12:00 pm to 12:30 pm

  • 23 September, 10:00 am to 10:30 am
Register once and choose the session time that best suits your schedule via Eventbrite: Introducing Apollo: USYD's New GPU Cluster for Research. 
No prior GPU experience is required.

AlphaFold3 now available on Apollo  

AlphaFold3 is now available for non-commercial use on Sydney University's Apollo GPU cluster. Researchers can move beyond the limitations of the public web server with support for custom molecules, co-factors, ligands, and non-standard residues, along with larger structure sizes and no daily job limits.
These capabilities unlock extended modelling of protein complexes, drug and metabolite interactions, enzyme function, post-translational modifications, and other molecular mechanisms that underpin biological systems. The service also supports high-throughput analyses and discovery workflows that would be impractical through the public web interface.
For information about accessing Apollo and running AlphaFold3, see: 
  • Access Apollo
  • How to run AlphaFold3 on the Apollo GPU Cluster 

Supporting Closing the Gap Through Statistical Capacity Building   

Over the past several years, Statistical Consultant Ali Green from the Sydney Informatics Hub has worked closely with the Bigiswun Kid research group led by Professor Elizabeth Elliott from the Faculty of Medicine and Health, supporting research that contributes to Closing the Gap initiatives in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities.
When the partnership began, the team primarily relied on the point-and-click software SPSS for their analyses. Through ongoing consultation, mentoring, and the development of reusable R-based workflows, they progressively gained the skills and confidence to conduct analyses in R, troubleshoot issues, and apply more advanced statistical methods. The transition to R provided greater flexibility, transparency, and reproducibility, supporting more sophisticated analyses while creating a foundation of reusable code that could be built upon as new research questions emerged. 
A key feature of this partnership has been its focus on building researcher independence. Rather than providing one-off analytical solutions, Ali worked alongside the researchers to strengthen their understanding of statistical concepts, programming, and analytical decision-making while developing practical workflows to support their research. The researchers increasingly ran analyses independently, assessed model assumptions, and selected appropriate modelling approaches without requiring the analysis to be conducted for them. Shared, reproducible code also encouraged peer learning, with team members troubleshooting and building on one another's work. As a result, consultations increasingly shifted towards collaboration, refinement, and addressing more complex methodological challenges.
The Bigiswun Kid partnership demonstrates the value of combining expert statistical support with researcher development. By building skills, confidence, and independence within research teams, this approach delivers benefits that extend well beyond individual projects. Great statistical consulting doesn't just solve today's problem, it helps researchers solve tomorrow's.
If you're interested in building statistical capability within your research group, we'd be delighted to discuss how the Sydney Informatics Hub can support you.

Sign-up for the GenAI tool staff pilot   

The University is preparing to pilot a soon-to-be-announced institution-wide generative AI tool, as part of our ongoing investment in responsible and equitable access to AI across research, education and operations.
We are seeking nominations from academic staff actively engaged in research who would like to take part in the staff pilot, commencing in late September 2026.
Participants will receive early access to the tool, opportunities to build their AI capability, and a direct say in shaping the broader staff and student rollout. The first release will focus on conversational chat capabilities, with more advanced features to be enabled progressively in stages throughout the rollout. You will help the University explore how generative AI can be used responsibly and purposefully in research and academic practice.
You can also express interest in becoming an AI Champion to lead your area by encouraging responsible experimentation, receiving early access to new functionality, and supporting colleagues as our AI capability continues to grow. 
Nominate yourself or a colleague - email ict.ai@sydney.edu.au by Friday, 11 September 2026.

Introducing New SIH Staff Member: Dr Nicole Dados, Digital Humanities Specialist

Sydney Informatics Hub recently welcomed Dr Nicole Dados to the team. Nicole is the Digital Humanities Specialist working on the Curated Collections project to support HASS and Indigenous researchers build accessible, sustainable and archivable digital collections. She is enthusiastic about showcasing the enduring value of humanities research and ensuring its long-term viability while also addressing the challenges arising from research intensification and the development of AI-powered tools.
Nicole initially trained in literature, languages and linguistics before going on to complete an interdisciplinary PhD focusing on urban social history at UTS. She has worked as a Research Associate on ARC and government-funded projects at the University of Sydney and UTS, and as a Postdoctoral Research Fellow at Macquarie University, undertaking quantitative, qualitative, mixed-methods, text-based, and biographical research.
In recent years, she has retrained in archives and information science, completing an internship at the State Archives of NSW and working as an Academic Support Librarian prior to joining SIH. Outside of work she enjoys historical fiction, family history research, baking and music, but is also a football enthusiast (or a supportive “soccer mum”) on the weekend.

Australian BioCommons Workshop: Long read sequencing

for bacterial genomics   

7 & 8 October: Zoom

Long read sequencing has been the go-to method in bacterial genomics, particularly to provide insight into how antimicrobial resistance genes spread throughout a population. The Australian BioCommons are running a free two-day online workshop (7-8 October 2026) led by expert bioinformaticians Michael (SIH) and Magda (QCIF).
The workshop will cover: 
  • The fundamentals of long read sequencing and how it differs from short read sequencing

  • Hands-on assembly of (ONT) bacterial genomes and identification of plasmids

  • Quality control and pre-processing analyses, and

  • Annotating the assembled genome to identify antimicrobial resistance genes of hospital-acquired infections 
This workshop is free, but participation is subject to application with selection.

GenAI Workshops 2026   

September & October: Zoom

Learn to use AI effectively in your research — without the guesswork.
Sydney Informatics Hub is running our GenAI workshop series again in September and October. Attended by over 1,600 researchers last year, these hands-on online workshops give you practical strategies for using AI across every stage of your research — from the basics through to data analysis, coding, and beyond. 
  • 21 September, 10am-12pm - GenAI 101: Fundamentals
Understand how large language models actually work, what their limits are, and how to choose the right AI tool for your research context — including running one locally on your own laptop.
  • 22 September, 10am-12pm - GenAI 201: Prompting
Learn how context and prompt structure shape AI outputs, and which prompting strategies to use — and avoid — depending on your task. 
  • 23 September, 10am-12pm - GenAI 202: Reading & Writing
Explore the tradeoffs of different AI strategies for finding papers, reading literature, and writing — so you can use them effectively without being misled by them. 
  • 12 October, 10am-12pm - GenAI 203: Tools
Cut through the noise — learn a practical framework for matching AI tools to your research tasks, and leave knowing exactly where each tool helps and where it doesn’t. 
  • 14 October, 2pm-4pm - GenAI 301: Data Analysis
Learn tool-agnostic strategies for using AI across your data analysis pipeline — and where to never let it take the wheel. 
  • 15 October, 10am-12pm - GenAI 303: Qualitative Analysis
Explore how AI can support qualitative research workflows — and where human judgement remains essential. 
  • 15 October, 2pm-4pm - GenAI 302: Coding
From understanding existing code to automating entire projects — learn how to use AI at every level of your coding workflow without losing control.
