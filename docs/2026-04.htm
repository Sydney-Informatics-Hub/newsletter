SIH hosts global nf-core hackathon to support scalable bioinformatics analyses  

The Sydney Informatics Hub recently hosted the Australian outpost of the global nf-core hackathon, joining 29 sites worldwide in a coordinated sprint to improve community bioinformatics workflows. The team was among the first to kick off the event, connecting with APAC collaborators across New Zealand, South Korea, and Vietnam. We were joined by twelve participants across the University of Sydney, University of New South Wales and the University of Melbourne, contributing to a collaborative and productive environment to advance multiple nf-core initiatives.
The group contributed to three projects that help lower the barriers for life scientists to run scalable analyses on HPC infrastructure. These included the preparation of a release for the nf-core ProteinFold pipeline, developed an easy-to-use utility, nf-core/tools ConfigBuilder, to simplify pipeline configuration for HPC, and prepare the Nextflow for HPC training materials for instructors to adopt and run independently.
We’d like to thank the teams at the Australian BioCommons, Seqera, and the nf-core community supporting the Australian outpost!

Sydney GPU Update 

In March, we undertook major upgrades to the Sydney GPU Cluster – a brand-new AI supercomputer with 24 x H200 NVIDIA GPUs for research use. The updates to the orchestration and control software are enabling us to improve the cluster’s usability and stability as we prepare for the official launch. While we continue to make improvements, researchers who would like to join the early-access program can submit a request here. The cluster is ready for free access for researchers while we are in pre-launch stage.
Please note that the cluster is not ready for storing and processing highly protected data yet.
Since opening early access to the Sydney GPU cluster, we’ve onboarded 40 research projects and granted access to over 130 users. Researchers are already running a diverse range of workloads, including LLM training, photonics simulations, and machine learning in R.
More info about the cluster, onboarding guide and documentation can be found here.

Connecting Classroom Learning with Real‑World Research Consulting

The SIH Statistical Consulting Unit recently had the wonderful opportunity to partner with academic colleagues and engage with senior Statistics students, creating a vibrant and engaging learning experience. Staff were invited to join a lecture as part of the elective Statistical Consulting Unit of Study, where students were eager to hear about our consultants’ career journeys and gain insights into consulting practice.
The discussion sparked thoughtful questions, with students particularly interested in understanding how statisticians approach real research problems and what skills are essential for effective consulting. Their enthusiasm was energising, especially as they begin working on real research questions sourced from across the University.
It was inspiring to see their curiosity and motivation to learn what statistical consulting looks like in practice. A big thank you to course coordinators Dr Jie Kang and Professor Jennifer Chan, and to Martin Huong, for facilitating this valuable opportunity. 

SIH Staff Profile: Dr Tim White, Data Science Software Engineer 

Tim is a Data Science Software Engineer at the Sydney Informatics Hub. After receiving his PhD in astronomy from the University of Sydney in 2014 he worked as a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Göttingen in Germany, Aarhus University in Denmark, and ANU, before return to Sydney in 2019 as a University of Sydney Fellow. In his previous research, he measured fundamental properties of stars, and developed data pipelines and novel data processing techniques for NASA’s Kepler and TESS missions.
Since joining SIH in 2022, Tim has diversified into a variety of new and interesting projects, such as contributing to a platform for geoscientists to share their models, and a website for reporting inherited diseases in animals. Most recently he has been working to prepare the Sydney GPU Cluster for use by researchers and is looking forward to seeing what users can achieve with this new supercomputer. He has also continued his involvement in astronomy research at the School of Physics by contributing to the onboard image acquisition and data processing software for the TOLIMAN Space Telescope, which, when launched, will search for planets in Alpha Centauri, our nearest neighbour star system. ‘Toliman’ is one of the traditional names for the star system, a Latinisation of the Arabic الظليمان, meaning ‘the ostriches.’
Outside of work, Tim enjoys playing piano, swimming, is an avid fan of pub trivia, and is a long-suffering supporter of the Parramatta Eels. Besides programming languages, he also likes to learn about the other kind of languages, most recently trying to pick up some Arabic. He's really not very good at it yet, but it's probably not as bad as his Danish.

Survival Analysis

5 May Hybrid workshop: in-person or Zoom

Across the University of Sydney researchers in
different disciplines are studying the time elapsed up
to when a specified event occurs.
For example, you might be interested in factors that influence survival of a person after a disease diagnosis or survival of a company after a stock market crash. Perhaps you like to study the survival time of marriage, or you are interested in how long a device or manufacturing component lasts before it fails or becomes obsolete. Survival analysis has also been applied to study staff churn or promotion, and the optimum time for soccer player substitution during a game.
To learn more about how ‘time-to event’/ survival analysis works, and how you can incorporate it into your research projects, sign up to the free Sydney Informatics Hub ‘Introduction to Survival Analysis’ workshop happening on May 5.

Meta-Analysis

5 May Hybrid workshop: in-person or Zoom

Are you currently working on a systematic review or plan to do so in the future? If yes, then it is important for you to know about meta-analysis, a statistical technique that can add a valuable quantitative component to your qualitative literature review. In contrast to primary studies that combine information across individual subjects, meta-analysis combines information across multiple studies. This powerful technique can provide a more precise estimate of the quantitative effect of a particular phenomenon, whether it be an educational intervention, drug treatment, disease incidence in the human population, or anything else. In addition to increased accuracy, meta-analysis provides a quantitative snapshot of the existing research in your field, enabling you to compare the contribution of individual studies to an overall estimate. You can use the technique to better understand conflicting results across multiple studies by examining potential publication bias, and the effect of different population or study subgroups.
To learn more about how meta-analysis works, and how you can incorporate it into your research projects, sign up to the free Sydney Informatics Hub meta-analysis workshop happening on May 5.

Linear Models 3 -Building Interpretable Models to

tell your research story

5 May Hybrid workshop:

in-person or Zoom

Statistical analysis is more than just building the best predictive model, it should also enable researchers to make the discoveries required to build new knowledge. Constructing engaging narratives about their research is also invaluable as researchers look to connect with their field, the community and funding bodies. To do this researchers need to test hypotheses, uncover unknown patterns, and present results in insightful, intuitive and memorable ways. In short, researchers need to build interpretable models. Our Linear Models 3 workshop to be delivered on May 5 explores tips and tricks to make your statistical analyses do just that.
Topics covered will be:
  • Reporting tricks that aid interpretation - estimated marginal means, confidence vs prediction intervals, applying and correcting for multiple comparisons, reporting variable ‘importance’, plus other reporting and interpretation tricks.
  • Model Parameterisation using the Design Matrix - interpreting categorical predictor parameters, dummy and effects coding.
  • More on Mixed Models - introducing the random slope.
