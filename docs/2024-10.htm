Launch of the APPN Node at Narrabri

On 11 September, the University of Sydney node of the Australian Plant Phenomics Network (APPN) was officially launched as part of the annual Plant Breeding Institute field day in Narrabri. The APPN is a National Collaborative Research Infrastructure (NCRIS) facility.
The focus is, not surprisingly, on plants, with the aim to enable rapid phenotyping of plants using sensing and data science in outdoor and indoor environments. The settings could be in classical plant breeding trials, commercial agriculture or for native vegetation, to name a few.  APPN is situated within SIH and is our first foray in providing physical infrastructure for researchers. This will include UAVs and ground-based platforms with LiDAR, multispectral and hyperspectral sensors. This will be augmented with handheld instruments such as LiCORs, spectrometers and field microscopes. The combination of these high throughput sensors and handheld instruments will enable precise data collection, with ground-truthing.  
It is the goal of the APPN to use this new infrastructure to capture high quality datasets.  This data will then be used in conjunction with artificial learning to pull out trait data that commercial partners and researchers both internally and externally are interested in.  Some initial traits of interest are photosynthetic potential, Nitrogen use efficiency, and plant mapping. However, the APPN has ambitious goals of supplying a catalogue of plant traits for researchers to draw from, with an efficient pipeline from data collection to publishable data.  
Pictured above (left to right) are SIH Director, Professor Thomas Bishop, APPN Senior Technical Officer Mr Connor Cassidy, APPN Director Dr William Salter, and the APPN CEO Richard Dickmann at the field day. William and Connor are based at Narrabri with an additional staff member being recruited. We also have Data Scientist, Jack Chan, based at SIH Sydney, with a second Data Scientist to be recruited.The focus of our node will be hyperspectral sensing and application of AI to plant science, which we believe have the most opportunity for impact.

University of Sydney supports development of NSW-wide statistical Guideline

The Animal Research Review Panel (ARRP) is the central body under the Animal Research Act (1985) to develop policy recommendations on the use of animals in research. Guidelines endorsed by ARRP are made freely available to NSW researchers, ethics committee members and research administrators alike to inform best practice in the use of animals for research. The ARRP has 12 members appointed by the Minister based on nominations received by industry, government and animal welfare groups.
In August 2024, ARRP published a new Guideline for use by Animal Ethics Committees on the determination of animal numbers and experimental design. Associate Professor Kieron Rooney, who is an ARRP and University of Sydney, Animal Ethics Committee member, led the development of the Guideline. The Animal Ethics team, in collaboration with the Sydney Informatics Hub's Statistical Consulting Unit (SIH SCU) provided feedback and comment on draft versions.
The Guideline makes ethics administration easier, supports optimal study design and promotes best practice in animal welfare standards by promoting the importance of study and statistical design and raising awareness of the Australian Code, and ARRIVE and NHMRC guidelines. Calculating optimal animal numbers ensures that projects produce robust and reproducible findings based on an appropriate study design and sufficient statistical power (i.e. enough sample) to detect effects of interest.
To receive support with study design and determination of appropriate animal numbers contact the Sydney Informatics Hub for free statistical training and one-on-one statistical consultancy.

The Australian computational structural biology community is live! 

Australian BioCommons has recently launched the computational structural biology community.  The community is being led by Dr Kate Michie from UNSW and involves structural biologists across many institutional partners across the country (including The University of Sydney).
We’re aiming to understand and address the computational challenges structural biologists face when using software like Alphafold and RFdiffusion, how national infrastructure can solve these problems, and how to make computational structural approaches more accessible to non-structural biologists. 
If you’d like to join the conversation, please visit the website and sign up for our quarterly community meetings, explore the guides, and keep track of upcoming events and other activities. Information on how to attend quarterly meetings and the topics being discussed can be found in the agenda.   

Easing the burden of bioinformatics workflows   

The rise of big data has made it essential to be able to analyse and perform experiments on large datasets in a portable and reproducible manner. 
SIH bioinformaticians are turning to Nextflow to make it easier to run data-intensive computational pipelines on commercial cloud and HPC.
September was a Nextflow-filled month for SIH and our colleagues at Australian BioCommons. We met with lead developer advocate, Geraldine Van Der Auwera, to discuss Australian community priorities and connections to their global community. We also announced the launch of the Australia Seqera Platform Service, and delivered an introductory Nextflow workshop to a national audience.  
You can find our Hello Nextflow workshop materials here and the webinar Geraldine delivered while in Australia, talking about the joys of Nextflow, on the Australian BioCommons YouTube channel.  

SIH's 5000th Ticket  

SIH have reached our 5000th project/consult ticket! The ticket is part of a research project led by Associate Professor Suzi Edwards, Academic Director of the Sydney Biomechanics Laboratory at the School of Health Sciences, and funded by the National Basketball Association.
The goal of the project is to identify game play events that lead to ankle sprain injuries and use state-of-the-art motion capture and machine learning to develop a computer vision model which can infer the 3D kinematics of player's ankle joints in real time from 2D video. SIH are providing statistics, data science, machine learning and software engineering support for this project, which is due for completion in 2025.

Operationalising Machine Learning Models with Vetiver: RLadies Sydney Recap 

International keynote speaker Julia Silge walked us through MLOps with the vetiver package during the latest RLadies Sydney meetup on September 6th, where we were joined by 47 guests! It was a fantastic session, packed with insights and practical applications. For those who couldn’t make it, the recording will be added to our website and YouTube page so watch this space!
In the meantime, here are three key takeaways from the event:
1. You Can Operationalise Your Own Models 
If you’ve developed a model, you already have the skills to operationalise it. In fact, you’re the ideal person to do so! MLOps—Machine Learning Operations refers to a set of practices that streamline the process of taking machine learning models from development to real-world application (called “production”).   
2. Tools Designed with Users in Mind 
Posit PBC, the creators of vetiver, always design tools with the end user front and centre. The vetiver package simplifies MLOps by allowing data scientists to easily deploy their machine learning models and keep them running smoothly. This package ensures you’re able to do the right thing with your data, making your machine learning operations more reliable and efficient. 
3. The Power of a Name 
The name vetiver is no coincidence. Vetiver is an oil known for its calming, stabilising qualities, often used in perfumes to balance volatility. Similarly, the vetiver package stabilises machine learning models, making them less volatile and more robust when they’re deployed into production. 
Keep in touch with RLadies Sydney for our next events, we’re already looking forward to our next session! 

SIH Staff Profile: Dr Cali Willet, Senior Research Bioinformatician

Locally known as “person zero”, Cali was the first employee of the fledgling Sydney Informatics Hub, then named Informatics Core Facility, commencing the bioinformatician role while completing her PhD within The University of Sydney, Faculty of Veterinary Science. Her PhD focused on animal genomics with an emphasis on rare variant discovery from high throughput sequencing technologies. 
At the outset of her role, she was instrumental in migrating researchers onto the then brand-new Artemis High Performance Computer, writing documentation and providing generalised and bioinformatics-focused Artemis HPC training. Now, much of her work is on NCI Gadi HPC, and she has developed numerous open-source efficient and scalable bioinformatics workflows optimised for enhanced throughput of large datasets on Gadi. She enjoys the diversity of projects at SIH, where she has worked on human genetics, cancer studies, livestock rare diseases, wildlife and companion animal genomics, and metagenomics to name a few. Recently completed projects include resistance to rust disease in a globally significant cereal crop, and the novel seekRNA genome editing pathway. She is currently collaborating with Monash Bioinformatics Platform and Australian BioCommons to deliver a genetics workshop on functional enrichment analysis, and looking forward to commencing a project on rare variant detection from long read sequencing for inherited human neuropathies. 
Cali lives on the beautiful NSW Central Coast, enjoys taking her two young children to the numerous beaches, and whenever possible, riding her horse Lucy (pictured)!

Linear Models 3

15 October Hybrid workshop, 11:30am-1:00pm: in-person or Zoom

Statistical analysis is more than just building the best predictive model, it should also enable researchers to make the discoveries required to build new knowledge. Constructing engaging narratives about their research is also invaluable as researchers look to connect with their field, the community and funding bodies. To do this researchers need to test hypotheses, uncover unknown patterns, and present results in insightful, intuitive and memorable ways. In short, researchers need to build interpretable models. Our Linear Models 3 workshop to be delivered on 15 October explores tips and tricks to make your statistical analyses do just that. Topics covered will be:
- Reporting tricks that aid interpretation - estimated marginal means, confidence vs prediction intervals, applying and correcting for multiple comparisons, reporting variable ‘importance’, plus other reporting and interpretation tricks
- Model Parameterisation using the Design Matrix - interpreting categorical predictor parameters, dummy and effects coding
- More on Mixed Models - introducing the random slope.
