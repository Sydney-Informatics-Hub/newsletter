Canine genomics provides hope for Australian racing greyhounds affected by ocular degeneration

In recent decades, veterinarians and geneticists have studied the genomes of domestic dogs to gain insight into the biological variation we naturally observe in mammals. This has included a large body of work dedicated to heritable diseases. Canine geneticists like Professor Claire Wade from the Faculty of Science take advantage of the unique population structure of dog breeds to reveal the genetic features that predispose some dog breeds to specific conditions.
In a recent publication in Genes, Professor Wade lead a team of researchers in confirming a genetic basis for chronic superficial keratitis (CKS) in the Australian racing greyhound. CKS is a progressive and inflammatory condition of the eye that can lead to blindness, if left untreated. Greyhounds are disproportionately affected by this disease compared with other dog breeds. Their results provide new and promising evidence which may help us understand the biological mechanisms responsible for this condition and potentially reduce its prevalence in greyhounds.  
The Sydney Informatics Hub supported this publication by processing the group’s whole genome sequence data. We used public and reproducible pipelines to perform mapping and germline variant calling at NCI’s Gadi high performance computer. You can find these pipelines and use them yourself at our Bioinformatics GitHub repository. 

Food prepared outside the home contributes to poor diet

Poor diet is a leading cause of non-communicable disease. Young Australian adults have poor diets with 36% of total energy intake coming from “unhealthy” discretionary foods. A team of researchers from the Nutrition and Dietetics Group, Sydney Nursing School, Charles Perkins Centre investigated risk factors for poor diet using wearable camera technology. This research was based on a collaboration with the Cancer Council NSW and researchers from New Zealand Universities.
Cameras were worn by 133 young adults and took images every 30 seconds for three consecutive days as participants went about their days. The cameras captured a total of 1,775 eating episodes that could be analysed. Consumed foods were coded as being either predominantly from the five food groups or discretionary. Lunch, dinner and snacks prepared outside the home were more likely to be discretionary. Males were more likely to have discretionary food for lunch and participants of lower socio-economic status were more likely to have discretionary food for breakfast and lunch. PhD candidate Virginia Chan led the publication of this work in the European Journal of Nutrition. The SIH Statistical Consulting unit assisted with advice about statistical design, analysis and interpretation of results.

The National Compute Merit Allocation Scheme 2023 (NCMAS)

The National Computational Merit Allocation Scheme (NCMAS) 2023 call for applications is now open and will close 2 October, 2022. This scheme will provide access to Australia’s largest high performance computing systems including the National Compute Infrastructure’s, Gadi, and the Pawsey Supercomputing Centre’s, Setonix. If your workflows have outgrown Artemis, cloud is too expensive, or you are looking for significant compute resources in 2023 this scheme is for you. ATAP quote extraction tool: lunchtime webinar, Thursday, 8th of September

An online notebook-based quotation extraction tool will be showcased in the first session of a series of SIH-hosted webinars, as part of the ARDC-supported Australian Text Analytics Project (ATAP).  
This tool allows researchers to find quotes in textual data, identify the speaker and what was said, including named entities. The tool does not require that researchers be able to code, and indeed is designed to be accessible for all.
The 1-hour online workshop (to be held at 12-1pm September 8th, Sydney time) is open to all across Australia, as well as NZ and beyond - and would be of interest to researchers from linguistics, computational social science, journalism and media studies, business, science and other faculties that work with textual data.

Research Software Engineering Asia Australia Unconference 2022

The first online Research Software Engineer Asia Australia Unconference will be held online from the 14th to the 16th of September. An unconference is an informal event where the agenda is decided by the participants, in the spirit of hacker events such as Foo Camp. RSE Asia Australia is a collaboration between the RSE Asia Association and the RSE Association of Australia and New Zealand. 
If you're a researcher who codes or an IT professional who supports research, the unconference is an opportunity to meet your peers, share tools and techniques and help to grow the research software engineering community. Registrations close on September 7.

Hacky Hour: Next session Wednesday, 21st of September

Hacky Hour is a regular meetup where all researchers – students, staff and university affiliates – gather in a social environment to collaborate and get research support. Experts and mentors from Sydney Informatics Hub and across the University will be available to advise and answer questions on coding, data analytics or digital tools.
Hacky Hour is held on the third Wednesday of every month: our upcoming sessions are at 3-4pm on the 21st of September and the 19th of October. You can join virtually via Zoom.
