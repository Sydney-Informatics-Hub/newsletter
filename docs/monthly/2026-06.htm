Making community workflows work for your research  

For many researchers working with large ‘omics datasets, the harder problem isn’t finding a pipeline - it is making one work for their specific data, computing environment, and research goals. Community workflows are powerful precisely because they’re built to be shared, but adapting them takes knowledge that’s rarely easy to acquire on the fly.
That gap is what a two-day national workshop was designed to close. “Unlocking nf-core: Customising workflows for your research” was delivered last week by Sydney Informatics Hub (SIH) bioinformaticians Dr. Michael Geaghan and Dr. Cali Willet, in partnership with Australian BioCommons. Thirty researchers from institutions across Australia joined online to work through the fundamentals of running and configuring a community-developed transcriptomics workflow.
The training materials are now publicly available and structured for self-paced use in your own compute environment.
Want to build these skills yourself? The SIH bioinformatics group’s Nextflow training series covers the foundations of high-throughput data processing, through to scaling workflows optimised for research computing infrastructure: 
  • Nextflow for the Life Sciences

  • Unlocking nf-core: Customising workflows for your research

  • Nextflow and HPC 

SIH Staff Profile: Dr Eden Zhang, Data Scientist 

Eden is a Data Scientist at the Sydney Informatics Hub (SIH). She holds a PhD in Environmental Microbiology from the University of New South Wales, conferred in 2022. Her doctoral research examined the impact of climate change on the diversity, drivers and dispersal of the East Antarctic soil microbiome, which includes fieldwork conducted at Casey Station during the 2019 austral summer season.
Since joining SIH in 2022, Eden has expanded her expertise across a diverse range of projects, such as: 
  • Working with AstraZeneca and researchers from the Woolcock Institute to investigate patterns of medication usage in individuals with mild asthma, with the aim of informing future clinical trial design.
  • Developing an application for epidemiological surveillance of gastrointestinal nematodes and antiparasitic drug resistance in ovines.
  • Working with researchers at the Gambling Treatment and Research Clinic to develop a machine learning workflow for predicting adverse gambling outcomes from online sports betting data, with findings recently published in the Journal of Addiction.
  • Together with other SIH team members, delivering Generative AI training to over 1,563 researchers in 2025. 
Most recently, she has developed an online screening tool to aid clinicians in the diagnosis of frontotemporal dementia, working with researchers at the Brain and Mind Centre and the FRONTIER research group.
Eden has a keen interest in data visualisation and UI/UX design and is committed to continuing her growth as a data scientist at the intersection of research, technology, and design. She is passionate about making complex data accessible and meaningful to diverse audiences.

Generative AI Essentials for Life Sciences 

1 July, 1:00-3:00pm: Zoom

As generative AI becomes an increasingly present tool in the research landscape, using it responsibly and effectively is a critical skill. This interactive workshop is designed for life science researchers who are new to generative AI or looking to enhance their approach.
You will learn to distinguish between commonly used generative AI models and align them to specific research tasks, discuss ethical risks and institutional policy obligations, and address data security and privacy considerations for research data. You will also get hands-on practice writing and refining system prompts tailored to your research area.
