Build your own digital lab with Galaxy Labs Engine  

If you are new to bioinformatics or moving into an unfamiliar domain, knowing where to start is often a key challenge. Our colleagues at Australian BioCommons have built "Galaxy Labs" to help guide researchers through the relevant tools, workflows and training materials for select domains, in a single easy-to-use web interface.
Existing labs currently cover microbiology, genomics, single cell omics, and proteomics analyses, each curated by a research community. If you'd like to curate your own set of tools and resources for your lab group or collaborators, the Galaxy Labs Engine lets you build and share a bespoke bioinformatics workspace without technical deployment experience required.
Explore Galaxy Labs on the Australian BioCommons website

Apollo GPU Cluster Update 

The Sydney GPU Cluster has been renamed Apollo. It’s a brand-new AI supercomputer with 24 x H200 NVIDIA GPUs for research use. Free, early access is still available for the month of July, before fee-for-use begins August 1 with very competitive subsidised rates. Find out more on the official documentation page.
This week we are updating the orchestration and control software are enabling us to improve the cluster’s usability and stability as we prepare for the official launch in September. While we continue to make improvements, researchers who would like to join the early-access program can add the Apollo GPU cluster to their DashR project here.
Since opening early access to the Sydney GPU cluster, we’ve onboarded 113 research projects and granted access to 333 researchers. Researchers are already running a diverse range of workloads, including AI model training, photonics simulations, and machine learning in R. 

Empowering Westmead Researchers  

Sydney Informatics Hub (SIH) is strengthening research capabilities at Westmead Hospital through expanded research support.
Recognizing the diverse statistical needs of researchers at Westmead, the SIH Statistical Consulting Unit (SCU) recently delivered foundational workshops on study design, statistical analysis plans and power & sample size on May 27. Attendees gained practical skills directly applicable to their research projects and benefited from expert guidance during a follow-up drop-in session.
This year, SIH has been offering regular biostatistical and bioinformatics drop-in sessions for Westmead-based researchers on the first Thursday of each month (12:00pm – 1:30pm) in K Block C24K, Level 6, Meeting Rooms 6117 Wagtail and 6118 Lyrebird. Starting in August, we're expanding this service to include research computing support.
Researchers are invited to connect with our experts for advice on research data, computing, and analysis.

22 July hybrid workshop:
in-person or Zoom

Do you have some research data but don’t know where to start with your analysis? Was your statistical training a long time ago or you really don’t feel confident about applying your statistical knowledge to your own data? Fret not, but sign up to the free Sydney Informatics Hub ‘Research Essentials - Analysing your Data’ workshop happening on 22 July.

Introduction to Survival Analysis

22 July hybrid workshop:

in-person or Zoom

Across the University of Sydney researchers in different disciplines are studying the time elapsed up to when a specified event occurs. For example, you might be interested in factors that influence survival of a person after a disease diagnosis or survival of a company after a stock market crash. Perhaps you like to study the survival time of marriage, or you are interested in how long a device or manufacturing component lasts before it fails or becomes obsolete. Survival analysis has also been applied to study staff churn or promotion, and the optimum time for soccer player substitution during a game.
To learn more about how ‘time-to event’/ survival analysis works, and how you can incorporate it into your research projects, sign up to the free Sydney Informatics Hub ‘Introduction to Survival Analysis’ workshop happening on 22 July.

Meta-Analysis: an introduction

22 July hybrid workshop:

in-person or Zoom

Are you currently working on a systematic review or plan to do so in the future? If yes, then it is important for you to know about meta-analysis, a statistical technique that can add a valuable quantitative component to your qualitative literature review. In contrast to primary studies that combine information across individual subjects, meta-analysis combines information across multiple studies. This powerful technique can provide a more precise estimate of the quantitative effect of a particular phenomenon, whether it be an educational intervention, drug treatment, disease incidence in the human population, or anything else. In addition to increased accuracy, meta-analysis provides a quantitative snapshot of the existing research in your field, enabling you to compare the contribution of individual studies to an overall estimate. You can use the technique to better understand conflicting results across multiple studies by examining potential publication bias, and the effect of different population or study subgroups. To learn more about how meta-analysis works, and how you can incorporate it into your research projects, sign up to the free Sydney Informatics Hub Meta-Analysis workshop happening on 22 July.
