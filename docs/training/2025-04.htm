Introduction to 
Survival Analysis

30 April Hybrid workshop: in-person or Zoom

Across the University of Sydney researchers in different disciplines are studying the time elapsed up to when a specified event occurs. For example, you might be interested in factors that influence survival of a person after a disease diagnosis or survival of a company after a stock market crash. Perhaps you like to study the survival time of marriage, or you are interested in how long a device or manufacturing component lasts before it fails or becomes obsolete. Survival analysis has also been applied to study staff churn or promotion, and the optimum time for soccer player substitution during a game.
To learn more about how ‘time-to event’/ survival analysis works, and how you can incorporate it into your research projects, sign up to the free Sydney Informatics Hub ‘Introduction to Survival Analysis’ workshop happening on April 30.

Meta-Analysis: an Introduction

30 April Hybrid workshop: in-person or Zoom

Are you currently working on a systematic review or plan to do so in the future? If yes, then it is important for you to know about meta-analysis, a statistical technique that can add a valuable quantitative component to your qualitative literature review. In contrast to primary studies that combine information across individual subjects, meta-analysis combines information across multiple studies.
This powerful technique can provide a more precise estimate of the quantitative effect of a particular phenomenon, whether it be an educational intervention, drug treatment, disease incidence in the human population, or anything else.
In addition to increased accuracy, meta-analysis provides a quantitative snapshot of the existing research in your field, enabling you to compare the contribution of individual studies to an overall estimate. You can use the technique to better understand conflicting results across multiple studies by examining potential publication bias, and the effect of different population or study subgroups.
To learn more about how meta-analysis works, and how you can incorporate it into your research projects, sign up to the free Sydney Informatics Hub Meta-Analysis workshop happening on 30 April.

Building Interpretable Models to tell your research story: Linear Models 3

30 April Hybrid workshop: in-person at or Zoom

Statistical analysis is more than just building the best predictive model, it should also enable researchers to make the discoveries required to build new knowledge. Constructing engaging narratives about their research is also invaluable as researchers look to connect with their field, the community and funding bodies. To do this researchers need to test hypotheses, uncover unknown patterns, and present results in insightful, intuitive and memorable ways. In short, researchers need to build interpretable models. Our Linear Models 3 workshop to be delivered on 30 April explores tips and tricks to make your statistical analyses do just that. 
Topics covered will be:
  • Reporting tricks that aid interpretation - estimated marginal means, confidence vs prediction intervals, applying and correcting for multiple comparisons, reporting variable ‘importance’, plus other reporting and interpretation tricks
  • Model Parameterisation using the Design Matrix - interpreting categorical predictor parameters, dummy and effects coding
  • More on Mixed Models - introducing the random slope
Check out our training calendar for more information and to register.

Showcasing the Possible: Call for GenAI Research Case Studies  

Have you seen the University's resources and training on Generative AI for research? Does it sometimes seem like everything is about what you shouldn't do - and very little showcases how GenAI tools can be - and are - being used for research?
We’d like to change that - so we're developing an online resource with case studies of how to do research "things" with GenAI - and we need your help!
Please tell us how you're using GenAI in your research via this short survey. Selected contributors will be invited to participate in a remunerated two-day in-person Co-Creation Session at the Camperdown campus on May 29-30. Attendees will discuss, share and collaborate to write about their experiences to develop GenAI use cases to share with the wider research community.
We're looking for concise case studies detailing practical applications of AI across the research lifecycle, including but not limited to: 
  • Literature search, review and synthesis 
  • Grant and manuscript preparation 
  • Experimental design and methodology 
  • Hypothesis generation and testing 
  • Data analysis, visualisation and interpretation 
Your submission should mention the tools employed, methodological approaches, challenges encountered, and outcomes achieved.
Our goal is to ensure that researchers from across the University of Sydney can responsibly use GenAI tools, to increase productivity and leverage the massive gains this technology enables - while staying safe and within University, journal and funding guidelines.
P.S. Can’t attend the session but have a use case you’d like to share? Please fill out the survey anyway – we’d love to learn and share what our community is doing!
