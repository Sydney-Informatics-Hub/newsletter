Survival Analysis

5 May Hybrid workshop:
in-person or Zoom

Across the University of Sydney researchers in different disciplines are studying the time elapsed up to when a specified event occurs. For example, you might be interested in factors that influence survival of a person after a disease diagnosis or survival of a company after a stock market crash. Perhaps you like to study the survival time of marriage, or you are interested in how long a device or manufacturing component lasts before it fails or becomes obsolete. Survival analysis has also been applied to study staff churn or promotion, and the optimum time for soccer player substitution during a game
To learn more about how ‘time-to event’/ survival analysis works, and how you can incorporate it into your research projects, sign up to the free Sydney Informatics Hub ‘Introduction to Survival Analysis’ workshop happening on May 5.

Meta-Analysis

5 May Hybrid workshop:
in-person or Zoom

Are you currently working on a systematic review or plan to do so in the future? If yes, then it is important for you to know about meta-analysis, a statistical technique that can add a valuable quantitative component to your qualitative literature review. In contrast to primary studies that combine information across individual subjects, meta-analysis combines information across multiple studies. This powerful technique can provide a more precise estimate of the quantitative effect of a particular phenomenon, whether it be an educational intervention, drug treatment, disease incidence in the human population, or anything else. In addition to increased accuracy, meta-analysis provides a quantitative snapshot of the existing research in your field, enabling you to compare the contribution of individual studies to an overall estimate. You can use the technique to better understand conflicting results across multiple studies by examining potential publication bias, and the effect of different population or study subgroups.
To learn more about how meta-analysis works, and how you can incorporate it into your research projects, sign up to the free Sydney Informatics Hub meta-analysis workshop happening on May 5.

Linear Models 3

5 May Hybrid workshop:
in-person or Zoom

Statistical analysis is more than just building the best predictive model, it should also enable researchers to make the discoveries required to build new knowledge. Constructing engaging narratives about their research is also invaluable as researchers look to connect with their field, the community and funding bodies. To do this researchers need to test hypotheses, uncover unknown patterns, and present results in insightful, intuitive and memorable ways. In short, researchers need to build interpretable models. Our Linear Models 3 workshop to be delivered on May 5 explores tips and tricks to make your statistical analyses do just that.
Topics covered will be:
  • Reporting tricks that aid interpretation - estimated marginal means, confidence vs prediction intervals, applying and correcting for multiple comparisons, reporting variable ‘importance’, plus other reporting and interpretation tricks.
  • Model Parameterisation using the Design Matrix - interpreting categorical predictor parameters, dummy and effects coding.
  • More on Mixed Models - introducing the random slope.

Unlocking nf-core: customising workflows for your research 

27 & 28 May workshop: Zoom

Processing large ‘omics datasets presents challenges for many life science researchers, particularly when workflows must be shared with collaborators and scaled across computing environments. Community-developed workflows such as those from nf-core provide reproducible and well-tested solutions for common analyses including RNA-seq, variant calling, mapping, and single-cell transcriptomics.
The workshop Unlocking nf-core: customising workflows for your research will introduce participants to the structure and tools behind nf-core pipelines and show how they can be adapted for different datasets and computational environments. Through hands-on examples using the nf-core/rnaseq workflow, participants will learn how to adjust parameters, configure resources, and customise workflow behaviour.
Delivered online by bioinformaticians Dr Cali Willet and Dr Michael Geaghan from the Sydney Informatics Hub, the two-day training will guide participants through the practical skills needed to run and adapt nf-core workflows. The workshop is aimed at researchers already working with, or planning to use, nf-core workflows in their research. Register today!

Pawsey call for PULSE Collaborations to accelerate research impact extended until 17 April

The Pawsey Supercomputing Research Centre has opened a new call for PULSE Collaborations (formerly Pawsey Uptake Projects), inviting Australian researchers to partner with Pawsey experts to accelerate the impact of their computational research.
The program supports projects that improve performance, scale, and efficiency using Pawsey’s infrastructure. Successful teams will receive up to 0.20 FTE of dedicated Pawsey staff support over six months to collaboratively improve research capability. Depending on the project’s needs, this support may draw on expertise from multiple Pawsey team members.
PULSE projects can span a wide range of activities, from benchmarking and code optimisation to GPU acceleration, workflow improvements, advanced visualisation, and hybrid quantum-classical approaches. Competitive applications clearly define their goals, outline team contributions, and effectively leverage systems such as Setonix, Australia's largest and most energy-efficient supercomputer.
Applications close 17 April 2026. For full details, eligibility criteria, and scope, visit the program page.
