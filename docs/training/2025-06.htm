Workshop: Nextflow for the life sciences

22-23 July, 1-5pm: Zoom

Join SIH bioinformaticians, Fred Jaya and Michael Geaghan for a hands-on introduction to Nextflow, a popular bioinformatics workflow management language. Nextflow makes it easy to run data-intensive computational pipelines, enabling scalable and reproducible workflows on any infrastructure.
SIH has been using Nextflow for the last few years to build scalable pipelines for national HPCs and is a member of the Nextflow Ambassador Community.
This workshop will put you on the path to writing your own workflows using Nextflow. You will learn how to use core components to build, run, and troubleshoot a scalable and multi-step workflow. The workshop will be run over 2 sessions across satellite sites across Australia, including from Sydney.
Join us on 22-23 July, 1-5pm AEST. The workshop is free to attend but applications are essential.
Applications close 27 June. See the Australian BioCommons website for more details.

Introduction to Survival Analysis

1 July Hybrid workshop:
in-person or Zoom

Across the University of Sydney researchers in different disciplines are studying the time elapsed up to when a specified event occurs. For example, you might be interested in factors that influence survival of a person after a disease diagnosis or survival of a company after a stock market crash. Perhaps you like to study the survival time of marriage, or you are interested in how long a device or manufacturing component lasts before it fails or becomes obsolete. Survival analysis has also been applied to study staff churn or promotion, and the optimum time for soccer player substitution during a game.
To learn more about how ‘time-to event’/ survival analysis works, and how you can incorporate it into your research projects, sign up to the free Sydney Informatics Hub ‘Introduction to Survival Analysis’ workshop happening on 1 July.

Meta-Analysis: an Introduction

1 July Hybrid workshop: in-person or Zoom

Are you currently working on a systematic review or plan to do so in the future? If yes, then it is important for you to know about meta-analysis, a statistical technique that can add a valuable quantitative component to your qualitative literature review. In contrast to primary studies that combine information across individual subjects, meta-analysis combines information across multiple studies.
This powerful technique can provide a more precise estimate of the quantitative effect of a particular phenomenon, whether it be an educational intervention, drug treatment, disease incidence in the human population, or anything else.
In addition to increased accuracy, meta-analysis provides a quantitative snapshot of the existing research in your field, enabling you to compare the contribution of individual studies to an overall estimate. You can use the technique to better understand conflicting results across multiple studies by examining potential publication bias, and the effect of different population or study subgroups.
To learn more about how meta-analysis works, and how you can incorporate it into your research projects, sign up to the free Sydney Informatics Hub Meta-Analysis workshop happening on 1 July.

Building Interpretable Models to tell your research story: Linear Models 3

1 July Hybrid workshop:
in-person or Zoom

Statistical analysis is more than just building the best predictive model, it should also enable researchers to make the discoveries required to build new knowledge. Constructing engaging narratives about their research is also invaluable as researchers look to connect with their field, the community and funding bodies. To do this researchers need to test hypotheses, uncover unknown patterns, and present results in insightful, intuitive and memorable ways. In short, researchers need to build interpretable models.
Our Linear Models 3 workshop, to be delivered on 1 July, explores tips and tricks to make your statistical analyses do just that.
Topics covered will be:
  • Reporting tricks that aid interpretation - estimated marginal means, confidence vs prediction intervals, applying and correcting for multiple comparisons, reporting variable ‘importance’, plus other reporting and interpretation tricks
  • Model Parameterisation using the Design Matrix - interpreting categorical predictor parameters, dummy and effects coding
  • More on Mixed Models - introducing the random slope.
