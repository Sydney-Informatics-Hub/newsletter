SIH's SCU at the Australian Statistical Conference 

The Statistical Consulting Unit (SCU) @ the Sydney Informatics Hub had an insightful week at the Australian Statistical Conference, hosted at Curtin University. This is Australasia’s premier statistical conference featuring a diverse range of topics such as AI, omics, data visualisation, machine learning, causal analysis (often more impactful than correlative analysis) & optimal study design, to name just a few. We attended keen to discover new tools to bring back to University of Sydney researchers that boost their impact and help secure them a seat at the table when society discusses its greatest challenges.
Kathrin Schemann and Chris Howden started our week off with a bang on Monday during an invited mentoring session on using Statistical Consulting to elevate research impact. Kathrin then talked about boosting research return on investment at the afternoon's invited session From Design to Compliance: Leveraging Biostatistical Expertise in Research.
Later in the week Alexandra (Ali) Green explained how AI is interacting with our goal to boost researcher's impact with Wait a Minute, is AI Making R and Subsequently Statistics Learning Harder Rather than Easier? Kathrin followed with Changing ‘Can’t Do’ to ‘How To’: Empowering Researchers by Fostering a Statistical Literacy Growth Mindset.
Chris explained how we give University of Sydney researchers the edge in a competitive research environment with Where’s the Hype for Causality? Unleashing the Power of Causation Over Correlation and Ali wrapped up the week by demonstrating how we facilitate our researcher’s real-world impact with Designing for Impact: How Teaching Study Design Shapes Causal Inference and Real-World Relevance. See this link for abstracts of all talks.
We are excited about bringing our learnings into our statistical consulting practice in 2026. Submit a request for statistical consultancy via the ‘Contact us’ form to get assistance!

Introducing New SIH Staff Member: Dr Minh Huynh, AI in Research Training Lead (Australian BioCommons)

Sydney Informatics Hub (SIH) recently welcomed Dr Minh Huynh to the team! Minh is the AI in Research Training Lead with Australian BioCommons, working with researchers to explore how AI can support life sciences research. She loves turning complex ideas into learning experiences that feel clear, practical, and approachable. A cell biologist by training, she spent over 10 years at Sydney Microscopy and Microanalysis looking at lots of tiny biological things before following her curiosity about how learning works into learning design. For the past four years, Minh has been a learning designer at the School of Life and Environmental Sciences (SOLES), Educational Innovation and the Library focusing on inclusive teaching practice, student experience, and mentoring. She has worked on projects around student transition, Universal Design for Learning, and generative AI in teaching and learning. Outside of work, she is a singer and performer, and she directs children’s choirs and musicals. It’s one of her favourite ways to build confidence and connection, and she tries to bring that same creative energy into her work.

Meta-Analyis

10 March Hybrid workshop:
in-person or Zoom

Are you currently working on a systematic review or plan to do so in the future? If yes, then it is important for you to know about meta-analysis, a statistical technique that can add a valuable quantitative component to your qualitative literature review. In contrast to primary studies that combine information across individual subjects, meta-analysis combines information across multiple studies. This powerful technique can provide a more precise estimate of the quantitative effect of a particular phenomenon, whether it be an educational intervention, drug treatment, disease incidence in the human population, or anything else. In addition to increased accuracy, meta-analysis provides a quantitative snapshot of the existing research in your field, enabling you to compare the contribution of individual studies to an overall estimate. You can use the technique to better understand conflicting results across multiple studies by examining potential publication bias, and the effect of different population or study subgroups.
To learn more about how meta-analysis works, and how you can incorporate it into your research projects, sign up to the free Sydney Informatics Hub Meta-Analysis workshop happening on Tuesday 10 March.
